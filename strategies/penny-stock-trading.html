Penny Stock Trading

Low-price, low-float stocks that can move enormous percentages on light volume — and can be manipulated just as easily.

What It Is & Why It's Volatile

Penny stocks trade at very low share prices (often under $5, sometimes fractions of a dollar) and frequently have a small "float" — the number of shares actually available for public trading. A small float means it doesn't take much buying or selling pressure to move the price dramatically: a modest dollar amount chasing a thinly-traded stock can send it up or down by double-digit percentages in minutes.

This combination of low price, low float, and often limited public information about the underlying company makes penny stocks among the most volatile and least predictable instruments a retail trader can access — and among the most susceptible to manipulation.

Typical Mechanics

  • Entry signals: an unusual spike in volume relative to the stock's normal (often tiny) average, frequently the first sign something — news, promotion, or manipulation — is happening.
  • Liquidity risk: the bid/ask spread can be wide, and there may not be enough buyers on the other side when you want to sell, especially in a fast-moving name.
  • Exit signals: extremely tight, active management — momentum can vanish as fast as it appeared, so many penny traders exit into strength rather than waiting for a target.
  • Holding period: usually minutes to a single session; holding penny stocks longer sharply increases exposure to gap risk and illiquidity.
  • Position sizing: should be small relative to account size specifically because of the elevated risk of a total, fast loss.

Walkthrough Example — ZAP

Zapline Energy (ZAP) is the academy's pump-and-dump-style sim: a sub-$2 price, extreme volatility (~130% annualized), and built-in large jump events that mimic sudden hype-driven spikes followed by equally sharp collapses.

On the Trade page, open ZAP's 1D chart. You'll likely see periods of relative calm punctuated by sudden, outsized spikes — exactly the pattern of a promotional pump followed by a dump once early buyers cash out. The lesson here isn't "how to catch the spike perfectly" (extremely hard even for professionals) — it's to feel firsthand how fast a small-account position can swing, and how dangerous it is to buy after a spike has already happened, chasing a move that may already be reversing.

Key Risks & Common Mistakes

Pump-and-dump schemes are a real and well-documented pattern in low-float, low-price stocks: promoters hype a stock to attract buyers, insiders sell into that demand, and the price collapses once promotion stops — often leaving late buyers with steep losses.
  • Chasing the spike: buying only after a big move has already happened, often right before the reversal.
  • Oversizing positions: treating a penny stock like a normal-sized position instead of a small, speculative allocation.
  • Trusting unverified hype: acting on social media tips or promotional messages without independent research.
  • Underestimating illiquidity: assuming you can exit instantly at the price you see — in real, thinly-traded names, large orders can move the price against you on the way out.
  • No stop discipline: in a stock this volatile, the absence of a firm exit plan can turn a small position into a large, fast loss.

Practice This Strategy

Run this drill on the trading desk with ZAP preselected:

  1. 1. Open ZAP's 1D chart and watch for at least a dozen ticks to observe its baseline volatility before doing anything.
  2. 2. Place a deliberately small Market Buy — a fraction of the size you'd use on a blue-chip sim — to respect the elevated risk.
  3. 3. If you notice a sudden spike after you're already in the position, consider selling into that strength rather than assuming it will continue.
  4. 4. If no spike occurs and the price simply drifts, practice exiting for a small loss or scratch rather than waiting indefinitely for one.
  5. 5. Review your Execution Log afterward and note how much faster ZAP's price moved tick-to-tick compared to a blue-chip sim like STDY.
