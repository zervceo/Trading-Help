Day Trading / Scalping

Rapid intraday entries and exits designed to capture small, frequent price moves before the market can reverse on you.

What It Is & Why It's Volatile

Day trading means opening and closing all positions within the same session — nothing is held overnight. Scalping is the fastest sub-style: trades that last seconds to a few minutes, targeting moves of a few cents to a few percent, repeated many times a day. Because the profit target per trade is small, scalpers gravitate toward the most volatile, highest-volume tickers, where price ranges wide enough in a short window to make the strategy worth the effort — and where liquidity is deep enough to get in and out without moving the price against yourself.

The volatility is the whole point: without fast, sizable intraday swings, there's no edge to scalp. But that same volatility means a scalper who is slow to exit can give back several trades' worth of gains in a single adverse tick.

Typical Mechanics

  • Entry signals: short-term momentum bursts, breaks above/below a recent intraday high or low, or a sudden spike in volume/volatility on the 1-minute chart.
  • Exit signals: a fixed profit target (often well under 1%), a tight stop-loss placed just beyond the entry candle, or simply the momentum stalling out.
  • Holding period: seconds to minutes — rarely more than 15–20 minutes, never overnight.
  • Position sizing: scalpers typically size larger per trade than swing traders (to make a small percentage move meaningful in dollar terms) but cap total risk per trade to a small fraction of account equity, because the strategy depends on a high win rate across many repeated trades rather than any single big winner.
  • Order types: market orders for speed on entry, though experienced scalpers often use tight limit orders to control exact fill price when the spread matters.

Walkthrough Example — VLTX

Voltix Robotics (VLTX) is one of the academy's high-volatility momentum sims (~65% annualized volatility), which makes it a realistic scalping sandbox — its 1-minute chart shows frequent multi-percent swings.

A typical scalp: watch the 1D chart on the Trade page. When VLTX ticks up sharply off a short base with rising momentum, buy a market order for, say, 100 shares. Set a mental stop a few ticks below your entry and a target roughly 1.5–2x your risk. If the price keeps pushing in your favor, sell into strength rather than waiting for a reversal. If it stalls or turns against you immediately, sell and take the small loss — don't let a scalp turn into an accidental swing trade.

Key Risks & Common Mistakes

Transaction costs and slippage matter enormously at this frequency — even with zero commission in this simulator, in real markets the bid/ask spread alone can erode a scalper's edge if trade counts are high.
  • Overtrading: forcing trades when there's no clean setup, just to stay active.
  • Moving the stop: widening a stop-loss after entry because "it'll come back" — the single most common way small losses become large ones.
  • Letting winners become losers: hesitating to take a fast, small profit and watching it round-trip back to breakeven or worse.
  • Revenge trading: immediately re-entering after a loss to "win it back," which compounds mistakes under emotional pressure.
  • Ignoring the bigger trend: scalping against a strong intraday trend increases the odds each individual scalp fails.

Practice This Strategy

Run this drill on the trading desk with VLTX preselected:

  1. 1. Open the 1D chart for VLTX and watch two or three price ticks go by to gauge current momentum.
  2. 2. When you see a short, sharp move in one direction, place a Market Buy for a small quantity (e.g. 25–100 shares).
  3. 3. Set a target in your head of roughly 1–2% above your fill price, and a "stop" of about 0.5–1% below it.
  4. 4. Watch the live price and mark price in your Open Positions table — the moment your target is hit, place a Market Sell to close.
  5. 5. If the price instead drops toward your mental stop, sell immediately rather than waiting for a bounce.
  6. 6. Repeat 3–4 times and review your Execution Log — how many trades were winners, and did you actually respect your own exit rules?
